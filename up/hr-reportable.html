When It's HR-Reportable

Some bosses are hard. Some cross a line. Once the conduct is reportable, your goal changes. You're no longer only trying to get along. You're building a record, protecting yourself and your team, and pushing back in ways that make your case stronger. This page is the playbook for that.

  • Separate the work from the conduct. Keep delivering and influencing on the work. Treat the conduct as a record-and-report problem.
  • The record is the case. Same day, facts only, exact words, witnesses, impact.
  • Get advice before you file: one consultation with an employment lawyer, and know your company's policy.
  • Counter in daylight. Every move should make your complaint stronger, not give the other side a story.

What Usually Counts

Policies and laws differ, so check your company's code of conduct. Most organizations treat these as reportable:

Usually Reportable
  • Racist, sexist, or other discriminatory remarks or "jokes"
  • Harassment, including repeated name-calling or insults
  • Threats or intimidation, including threats to your job for raising concerns
  • Retaliation against you or someone else for speaking up
  • Repeated public humiliation that targets a person
  • Pressure to break law, policy, or safety rules
Usually Just Difficult
  • Bluntness, impatience, or high standards applied to everyone
  • Changing priorities, unreasonable deadlines
  • A single bad day, followed by an apology or a reset
  • A management style you dislike but that isn't personal

Difficult behavior is still worth logging. Patterns change category over time.

Build the Record

  1. Log Every Incident, Same DayUse the Interaction Debrief. Write the date, setting, who was present, exact words, what happened, the impact, and what you did. Tag it: name-calling, discriminatory remark, possible retaliation.
  2. Recap Decisions in WritingA 1:1 recap email after every meeting. Recaps are dated, they're sent through company systems, and they're hard to deny later.
  3. Name the WitnessesNote who saw it. Don't pressure anyone to back you up. Just record who was there.
  4. Keep Your Own Notes OnlyNever copy confidential company documents or other people's private messages. Keep your notes on a personal device where policy allows, and back them up.
  5. Log Your Wins TooA clean performance record is your best defense if anyone later claims the complaint is about your performance. Keep a wins file.

Before You File

  • Read your company's code of conduct, and its anti-harassment and anti-retaliation policies. Note the reporting channels: HR, an ethics line, a skip-level.
  • Book one consultation with an employment lawyer. Many offer a short first call, and it's worth it before a formal complaint.
  • Check the law where you work before recording any conversation. Some places require everyone's consent. When in doubt, don't record, and write it down instead.
  • Remember what HR is for: protecting the company. A documented pattern of reportable conduct is a risk the company has to address, so a factual, pattern-based complaint lines up your interests with theirs.
  • Know what you're asking for: the behavior to stop, an investigation, a change in reporting line, protection from retaliation.
  • Line up support outside work: a partner, a friend, a therapist, your Employee Assistance Program.
  • Update your career insurance. Options make you calmer, and calm makes you more credible.

Filing

Put it in writing, even if you also meet in person. Keep it factual and pattern-based: no adjectives about the person, just dates, words, witnesses, and impact. Use the written HR complaint template, attach the timeline below, and keep a copy of everything you send. After any HR meeting, send a short summary of what was said (HR meeting prep).

Retaliation Watch

Retaliation for raising a good-faith concern is prohibited in many places and by most company policies. Watch for changes that start after you raise a concern, and log each one with a date:

Work Changes

Being removed from projects, meetings, or threads. Your best work reassigned. Suddenly impossible targets.

Record Changes

A sudden negative review, a surprise performance plan, or criticism that doesn't match your record.

Treatment Changes

Being frozen out, or more hostility, or your team being targeted.

Tag these as Possible retaliation in the Debrief, tell HR in writing, and if it continues, go outside the company: an employment lawyer, or the government agency that handles workplace complaints where you live.

Countering While a Case Is Open

You don't have to go quiet. You have to stay in daylight. Every counter should pass the Daylight Test, plus one more question: does this make my complaint stronger or weaker?

Strengthens Your Case
  • Recap every decision in writing, calmly
  • Hold hard conversations in group settings or with a witness, or move them to email
  • Make your team's results visible to your skip-level and peers (how)
  • Line up allies: peers who've seen the pattern, a mentor, a sponsor
  • Use calm boundary scripts in the moment
  • Keep delivering. A clean record is hard to argue with.
  • Protect and repair your team (rebuild)
Weakens Your Case
  • Anything that looks like retaliation or a smear
  • Venting on company chat, or gossiping about the case
  • Leaking documents, or forwarding confidential material to yourself
  • Recording without consent where that's illegal
  • Matching their tone in public
  • Letting your own deliverables slip
  • Pressuring witnesses
The Counters That Work Best

Counter-Spells for credit-taking, isolation, and rewriting history. SPELL for building your position: Say less, Position, Earn leverage, L1 Line up allies, L2 Land on timing. ARC for impossible demands. All of them leave a paper trail that helps you.

Not Legal Advice

This page is general information, not legal or HR advice. Employment law varies by country and state. For your specific situation, talk to an employment lawyer. If you feel unsafe or in crisis, contact local emergency services. In the US, call or text 988.
