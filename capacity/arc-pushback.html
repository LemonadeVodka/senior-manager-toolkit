ARC Pushback

Leaders don't push back by refusing. They push back by making the trade-off visible and putting the decision with the person who owns the priority. ARC is a three-step way to do that without sounding defensive, and it works on bosses, peers, and executives alike.

The ARC Framework

AAcknowledgeShow you understand the goal and why it matters to them.
RRevealMake the trade-off visible, with facts: what slips, what breaks, what it costs.
CChooseOffer options and your recommendation, then let them decide.
ExampleARC in Action
A: "I understand — the exec review is Thursday and you need the reliability numbers."
R: "To pull them together properly, I'd pause the vendor renewal analysis, which is due Friday.
    Or I can give you a lighter version with last month's data."
C: "My recommendation is the lighter version now, the full version next week.
    Which works better for you?"

Three Shapes of Pushback

ShapeUse whenSounds like
Yes, if…You can do it, with a condition"Yes, if we move the vendor review to next week."
Not now, but…Right idea, wrong time"Not this sprint, but I can start it on the 15th, or scope a first step now."
No, and here's what I can doIt shouldn't be done, or not by you"I don't think my team should own this, because… Here's who's better placed, and I'll make the intro."

Weak / Good / Best

Weak

"We're slammed, I can't." (Sounds like complaining.) Or a silent yes, followed by a quiet miss.

Good

"Happy to. That means X slips to next week. Is that OK?"

Best

Acknowledge, reveal the trade-off with data, offer two or three options and a recommendation, and confirm the decision in writing.

With a Difficult Boss

  • Do it in writing when they're volatile: they get time to cool off, and you get a record.
  • Lead with their goal, not your workload. Hearing that you're "too busy" lands as weakness. Hearing about their risk lands as partnership.
  • Make the default explicit: "If I don't hear otherwise by tomorrow, I'll prioritize A."
  • Don't over-explain. Long justifications invite debate. State the trade-off and stop.

Force-Rank: When Everything Is a Priority

Level 2 Force-Rank. The rule: two #1s means no #1. Use it when your boss calls everything a priority. The written ranking becomes your shield when something low on the list slips.

If your boss calls everything a priority, nothing is. Don't argue about whether each item matters. Make them rank.

  1. List ThemWrite every current "priority" in one place: the list, not the debate.
  2. Ask for the Rank"These are all important. If we can only finish one this week, which is #1? Then #2?"
  3. Hold the Line on Ties"Two #1s means no #1. Which one wins if they collide?"
  4. Confirm in WritingSend the ranked list back (template). It becomes the reference for every new ask.
  5. Slot New AsksWhen a new "priority" arrives: "Where does this go in the ranking? It'll push out #3."
The Ranked List Is Your Shield

When you're blamed for something low on the list slipping, the written ranking shows it was a decision, not a failure.
