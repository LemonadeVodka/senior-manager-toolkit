PROVE

You don't need to be the best engineer in the room to be the most useful person in it. You need to know whether the team has thought clearly. Ask them to PROVE it: five questions that work on any proposal, design, estimate, or incident, and that a technical boss can't dismiss as fluff.

The Five Questions

PProblemWhat, for whom, and how do we know? RRiskWhat breaks, and how would we know? OOptionsWhat else did we consider? VValueWorth it, after build, run, and maintain? EEvidenceWhat data supports this?
QuestionAskGood answerWeak answer
Problem"What problem does this solve, for whom? How do we know it's real, and how big is it?"A user or business impact, with numbers"It's best practice." "The old one is ugly."
Risk"What could go wrong? How far would it spread? How would we find out? How do we roll back?"Named failure modes, monitoring, a tested rollback"It should be fine." No rollback plan.
Options"What else did you consider? Why not those? What happens if we do nothing?"Two or three alternatives, with clear reasons"There's only one way to do it."
Value"What does it cost to build, run, and maintain, and who carries it? Is the benefit worth that?"Build time plus ongoing operations, on-call, and toil, set against a clear benefitOnly the build cost, or "it's free, it's open source"
Evidence"What data, test, or prototype supports this? What would change your mind?"Benchmarks, a pilot, metrics, a clear way to be proven wrong"Trust me." "Everyone does it."

With a Technical Boss Who Tests You

  • Ask, don't assert. A precise question shows judgment without inviting a contest over who knows more.
  • Ask the team before the meeting, so your team walks in with answers, not you walking in with guesses.
  • When you don't know, say so and follow up: "I don't know yet. I'll have the number by Thursday." Then send it. Delivered follow-ups build credibility faster than clever answers.
  • Follow up with the ladder when an answer is thin: show me → what's normal → where → what else → what would you change (How to Ask).
