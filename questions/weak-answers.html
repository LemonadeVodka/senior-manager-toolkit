Pillar 4 · Lead With Questions

Spotting a Weak Answer

You don't need to judge the technology to judge the thinking. Weak answers have recognizable shapes. When you hear one, don't challenge the person. Ask the follow-up that invites a stronger answer.

Red Flags and Follow-Ups

You hearIt might meanFollow up with
"It should be fine."Untested assumption"What would tell us early if it isn't?"
"Everyone does it this way."No analysis of our context"What's different about our situation that might matter?"
"Trust me."No evidence, or no time to gather it"What's the quickest way we could check?"
"There's only one way."Options weren't explored"If that weren't possible, what would you do?"
A wall of jargonUnclear thinking, or protecting turf"Can you explain it the way you'd tell an executive?"
No rollback planHigh risk"If this goes wrong at 2 a.m., what do we do?"
"It's 90% done" for weeksHidden blockers, or unclear scope"What's the last 10%, specifically? What's in the way?"
Only the build costHidden ongoing cost"Who maintains it, and what does that cost each year?"
Confident date, no rangesFalse precision"What's the best case and the worst case?"

What Strong Answers Sound Like

  • They include numbers: how many, how often, how fast.
  • They name uncertainty: "We're not sure yet, and here's how we'll find out."
  • They mention alternatives, and why they weren't chosen.
  • They describe how we'd know if it's working or failing.
  • They're explainable in plain language.
"I Don't Know" Is a Good Answer

Reward it. "Good, how would we find out?" teaches the team that honesty is safer than bluffing, and that protects you from surprises.
