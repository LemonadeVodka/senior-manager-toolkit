Documentation Done Right

Good documentation is insurance. You hope you'll never need it, and if you do, it's invaluable. It also helps you see patterns clearly and stop doubting your memory. Done wrong, it can hurt you. Here's how to do it right.

The Rules

  • Write it the same day, while it's fresh. Notes made at the time carry more weight.
  • Facts only: date, time, place, who was present, exact words where you remember them, what happened, impact.
  • Your own notes only. Never copy company confidential documents, data, or other people's private messages.
  • Use a personal device and account, and keep it private. Work systems may be monitored or lost if you leave.
  • Don't record conversations secretly. It's illegal in some places without consent, and it can destroy trust and your case.
  • Keep the good too. Positive interactions make your record credible and fair.
  • Save written evidence you're entitled to, such as emails sent to you, but follow company policy on forwarding.

What a Good Entry Looks Like

ExampleLog Entry
Date / time: [YYYY-MM-DD, 10:05]
Setting / present: Weekly review (video), 6 people incl. two of my reports
What happened: Before we finished presenting, [boss] said "this is amateur work"
  and moved to the next topic without questions.
Impact: Two engineers stopped contributing; one messaged me afterwards, upset.
My response: Said I'd follow up 1:1. Sent recap of decisions at 11:00.
Pattern: Public criticism (3rd time this month)

Use the private Interaction Debrief tool or the log template.

What Documentation Is For

  • Clarity: seeing whether there's really a pattern.
  • Conversations: specific examples for upward SBI or a skip-level conversation.
  • Escalation: a factual timeline for HR (ladder).
  • Protection: if your performance or conduct is ever questioned unfairly.
Check Local Rules

Employment and privacy laws differ by country and state. If you think you may need legal protection, a short consultation with an employment lawyer is worth it.
